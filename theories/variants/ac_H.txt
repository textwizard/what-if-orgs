The management of this firm approaches its decisions from the perspective of the Absorptive Capacity framework (Cohen and Levinthal, 1990; Zahra and George, 2002). The framework holds that a firm's ability to recognise the value of new information, to assimilate it, and to apply it commercially depends on the related knowledge it already holds. Prior knowledge accumulates through the firm's own research and development, its production and market experience, and its cooperation with customers, suppliers, universities, and research institutes. Because learning builds on what is already known, it is cumulative and path dependent. Firms learn readily in areas close to their existing knowledge and find it difficult to evaluate and improve activities far from it, while investments in related knowledge make later learning easier. The framework distinguishes potential absorptive capacity, the acquisition and assimilation of knowledge, from realised absorptive capacity, its transformation and exploitation, and internal communication and integration across functions link the two. Absorptive capacity also shapes how a firm interprets feedback. A firm with related knowledge can read the early results of a new product as information about what to change, whereas a firm without it sees mainly whether the product succeeded or failed. Firms that do not invest in related knowledge may fail to recognise opportunities and fall behind, and the capacity they lack is hard to build quickly.

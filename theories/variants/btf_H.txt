The management of this firm approaches its decisions from the perspective of the Behavioral Theory of the Firm (Cyert and March, 1963; Greve, 2003). The theory views the firm as a coalition of boundedly rational decision makers who attend to a few goals at a time and judge results against aspiration levels rather than optimising. Aspirations form from two sources, the firm's own past performance and the performance of comparable firms, and they adapt to experience, so that a result that once disappointed can become acceptable over time. Performance feedback guides behaviour. When results fall below aspiration, management engages in problemistic search, which starts close to the symptom and to existing routines and moves to more distant alternatives only as nearby solutions fail. A shortfall also raises the willingness to accept risk in order to close the gap, while results near or above aspiration favour the continuation of established routines. Organisational slack, the resources beyond what current operations require, buffers the firm against shortfalls, allows it to carry uncertain initiatives, and funds search that is driven by opportunity rather than by a problem. Without slack, shortfalls draw attention more sharply and resources are reallocated more quickly. Because attention is limited, management attends to the goals and products that are most salient, and its decisions follow standard operating procedures learned from earlier experience. What the firm learns from experience is itself shaped by these mechanisms, since a firm learns little about an activity it no longer pursues.
